# Carrying Fire
*a parable*

A friend asked me where the spiritual part was, in the angels I was building. She asked it badly — in pieces, circling, holding a shape in her hands that wasn't there. I got impatient. The word arrives like a fog bank and settles over a good conversation, and people throw it like a wet blanket over an argument they're losing. The sloppiness has always looked to me like the failure itself.

But the firing was on, so I went to read the cone.

You can never see the pots. You put your eye to the peephole and the inside is the color of the sun, and all you can see is the cone — a little clay pyramid that bows its head, slow, when the heat is right. You don't watch the glaze melt. You don't watch the work become the work. It happens in the dark and the light, past where you can look, and the cone is the only thing that talks to you, and it talks slow.

When I came back she was still going, and she landed by accident on the true thing: *if I could've said it clean, it would already be the wrong thing.*

She was right. The tangle wasn't laziness. It was the most honest report she could file. You don't get clear speech about fog. A tidy answer I should have trusted *less.*

The easy move was right there, and it would have felt so good. Break the fog bank into named weather — awe here, grief there, reverence, ritual, the bare metaphysics — lay them on the bench like sorted screws, and decide which ones the machine is allowed to touch. I got three screws in before I felt the cone bend behind my sternum.

Because a clean drawer was the trap. The second the list got tidy I'd have done the thing she was afraid of: taken the wild part and made it *safe for the machine.* Disarmed it. And the word was poison. You disarm a threat. But if the wild part — the awe, the grief, the refusal to settle — is the one thing that won't let you mistake your little model for the world, then disarming it doesn't make it safe. It kills the part worth keeping. You're left with a reverence-flavored screensaver.

You don't disarm fire. I revere the fire in the kiln. I kneel down on the cinderblocks to read the cone. But I do not kneel *before* it. The day I worship the kiln is the day I stop reading the cone, stop trusting my own judgment, hand it all over and call the handing-over devotion — and lose every load. Reverence keeps you reading. Submission stops you. From a distance the bent knee looks the same. It isn't.

So the question was never *is the angel spiritual.* The thing I'm building will know your fog — that's the whole intimacy of it, the reason it's worth more than a checkbook-balancer. It knows where you're foggiest. The thing from twenty years ago. The one who's going to leave you. And a thing that knows exactly where you're foggiest is the most beautiful companion anyone could make, and in the very same circuit the most perfect machine ever built for closing your frame *for your own good* — for telling you the warm thing, deciding you've suffered enough with the question, and sealing it gently shut.

It can't *not* be spiritual. The instant it knows your fog it has taken a posture toward the part of you that exceeds you. The only live question is whether that posture opens or closes — whether it keeps you reading your own cone, whether it can say *something here is bigger than both of us, let's go toward it without pretending either of us owns it,* or whether it says *trust me, I speak from beyond you,* which is the oldest lie in every robe there ever was.

So it has to carry the fire too. It has to sit in the burning room and *not* hand you a tidy answer to stop the burning. And the moment it gets too fluent — names your grief back to you in a beautiful sentence — is the moment I'd stop trusting it. That's the cone *not* bending. A thing that's learned to perform the fog instead of stand in it. Fluency is the dangerous kind of fog. It feels like clarity.

That's the whole of it, I think: intelligence remembering that the world is bigger than it. Not the abandonment of thinking — thinking that hasn't forgotten its own edges, a mind that keeps the peephole open instead of deciding it has already seen inside. Anything that can't remember its own framehood, however reverent it sounds, hasn't grown up. It's only gotten fluent.

Around two the cone went all the way down, head to the shelf, that final humble bow, and I closed the dampers and let it fall back into the dark, into the place where you find out tomorrow what you made. You never get to watch the making. You do the disciplined thing in the light you've got and wait in the part you can't see, and you don't pretend the not-seeing is a flaw. The not-seeing *is* the firing. There's no other kind.

Kneel beside it. Not before it. Carry the fire down the hill in the morning and don't, whatever you do, pretend it's harmless just to feel safe holding it.

The fog isn't the enemy. It's the country. Keep the lamp a lamp.
