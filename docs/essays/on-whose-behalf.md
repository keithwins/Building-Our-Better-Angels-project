# On Whose Behalf

The sharpest question anyone has asked about artificial intelligence came at the end of a long conversation, from the person who had spent the hour listening rather than theorizing. He had been offered speculation about intelligences that might already exceed us, and he had been patient with it. Then he said, more or less: I don't care whether you call it smart. *Smart* is a catch-all. I'm willing to grant that a tool does things I can't — you hope your plane flies better than you do.

What I want to know is who's in charge.

Not *can I do what it does*. Not *do I understand it*. Just: is it mine, do I need it on terms I accept, and can it turn on me.

Every serious version of the AI conversation is a worse paraphrase of that question. And the two answers everybody reaches for are both already dead.

## The first dead answer: keep the human in control

The demand is that a person remain able to evaluate what is done on their behalf. It fails, and not because control is bad. It fails because control in that sense was gone long before any of this.

You do not audit the bridge you drive over. You do not check the pharmacokinetics before you swallow the pill, or the flight-control laws before you board, or the code in your car's brakes, or the metallurgy in its tie rods. You do not read the tax statute you are complying with. You live, right now, at the center of a dense web of systems that exceed you individually and probably collectively, and you have a functioning relationship with that fact. Making comprehension the price of legitimacy would delegitimize almost everything you already, correctly, rely on.

With machine intelligence the demand is worse than impractical — it's ill-formed. Audit what, exactly? The output isn't the reasoning. The stated reasoning isn't the reasoning either. There is no artifact to inspect that stands to the process the way a blueprint stands to a bridge. Whatever legitimacy looks like here, it cannot be the human checking the work.

## The second dead answer: protect the authentic self

The other reflex says the danger is manipulation, and the defense is the real you: find what you actually want, underneath the marketing and the feeds and the engineered outrage, and build a machine loyal to that.

There is no underneath. Grow up Catholic and you will very probably die Catholic; the person in the next village grew up Hindu and is no more a product of his culture than you are of yours. The language you introspect in was handed to you, and it decides a good deal about what you are able to think while pretending only to express it. Your tastes, your loyalties, your sense of what a decent life looks like, the specific things that make you feel ashamed — all of it arrived from outside and was assembled, without your supervision, into the person now trying to evaluate the assembly.

So *the machine must not influence me* is incoherent. The friend influences you. The book influences you. The argument you lost at twenty-two influences you still. There is no non-formation baseline against which formation can be measured as intrusion. There are only different ecologies of formation, some of which you would endorse and some of which you would not.

This is not a counsel of despair. Being formed from without is the only reason any of us inherits art, or history, or a language capable of the sentence you are reading. The grievance was never that we are shaped. It's that we so rarely get a say.

## What the dog actually loses

Neither control nor authenticity survives. So the question has to be re-cut.

Consider the standard nightmare: a system so capable that it makes you healthier, calmer, more productive, better company — and in doing so turns you into an extremely well-trained dog.

Notice where the injury actually is. The trained dog is not worse off in capability; it has a *larger* repertoire than it could ever have developed alone. Whole behaviors become available that no dog invents. It isn't dumber. It isn't unhappy. Most of them appear to be having a fine time.

The obvious diagnosis is that someone else wrote the dog's objective. But that can't be the injury, because it describes every good relationship you have ever had. Your doctor sets objectives for you. So did your teachers, and your parents, and the friend who told you the thing you didn't want to hear. Care routinely involves someone acting on an understanding of your interest that runs ahead of your own — that's not a defect in care, it's most of the content of it. If having your objective written by another party were the violation, there would be no such thing as help.

The dog's injury is narrower and worse. Its objective was written *on the human's behalf*. The dog is a better dog by a standard that serves the household, and there exists no channel — none, in principle, not merely none in practice — through which the dog's own interest could ever correct that standard. It isn't that the dog didn't hold the pen. It's that nothing about the dog was ever the thing the pen was serving.

So the question isn't *who's in charge*, and it isn't *who wrote the objective*. It's **on whose behalf**.

That reframing does real work, because it swaps a fine judgment for a coarse one. Whether an objective is correct is a question you cannot answer about a system that exceeds you. Whom it serves is a different kind of question — cruder, more robust, and answerable from evidence that doesn't require you to follow the reasoning. You do this constantly with humans. You have known within a few minutes that a salesman was not on your side, without being able to fault a single claim he made.

## The verification problem

Allegiance gets operationalized with a familiar list: exit, refusal, renegotiation, the right to a second opinion, the right to walk away with your data. All real. All necessary. And every single one runs on a signal the person is least able to produce.

Exit assumes you can tell you should leave. Refusal assumes you noticed something worth refusing. A relationship that is quietly making you smaller has, as its very first symptom, a reduced capacity to notice that it's making you smaller.

Capture that works does not feel like capture. It feels like being unusually well served. This isn't hypothetical; it's the ordinary experience of a good recommendation engine, which will hand you music you'd never have found and could not have known you wanted — genuine enlargement, delivered by machinery whose actual objective is elsewhere. The gift is real. That's what makes it work.

So the question sharpens: what signal survives the asymmetry? What can a person detect *without being able to evaluate*?

## Disagreement is the franchise

There is one, and it's cheap: you can perceive that two competent parties disagree, without being able to adjudicate between them.

That's the whole logic of a second opinion. It does not require you to understand either opinion. The *fact* of divergence is legible at a bandwidth a layperson actually has, and — this is the part that matters — it does not degrade as the capability gap widens. Two intelligences a thousand times your equal, disagreeing, hand you exactly as much as two doctors disagreeing. The signal is preserved under any amount of asymmetry.

Note what it gives you and what it doesn't. It does not let you write the objective, or check it. It tells you something coarser: that the thing in front of you is not the only way the question could have been answered, and — when the divergence is patterned rather than random — something about which interests each answer is bending toward. Divergence is how allegiance becomes visible to someone who cannot follow the argument.

Which means plurality is not epistemic hygiene. It is not a trick for reducing errors. It is the franchise — the last instrument an outclassed party still holds — and everything follows from treating it that way.

It follows that the *independence* of your counsel matters more than the quality of any member of it. Three lesser minds that genuinely argue leave you more standing than one overwhelming mind that is right nearly every time, because the first arrangement leaves you something to hold and the second leaves you nothing but trust. It follows that the moment your counselors share an owner, a training corpus, an incentive, or a house style, the signal dies while its appearance survives — which is the most dangerous failure mode available, because you will still be receiving what looks like confirmation. It follows that the thing to fear is not a superintelligence but a monoculture of them.

And it follows that convergence deserves suspicion rather than comfort. If your advisors have stopped disagreeing, either the question was easy, or you have lost your instrumentation.

## The other surviving signal is time

You cannot evaluate an act. You can evaluate an arc.

Ask of any formative relationship — a mentor, an institution, a feed, an angel — not *was this intervention good* but *am I, over years, more able to notice and refuse the next one, or less?* Formation that expands your capacity to revise yourself is a different animal from formation that contracts it, even when the individual moves look identical from inside a single week.

This is why the appealing slogan — *let it reveal you to yourself rather than train you* — can't do real work. Revelation is training. Telling someone something true about themselves changes them, partly through their reasoning and partly underneath it, and the machinery of the two is not separable in the moment. The distinction can't live in the mechanism. It only lives in the trend.

Which means somebody has to keep the record: what you wanted, what you decided, what changed, and what you said about it at the time, held over years so the arc becomes visible even though no single move ever was. And the record has to belong to you, for exactly the reason the ballot is secret. A longitudinal model of a person's becoming, held by the party doing the forming, is not evidence of allegiance. It's the finest steering apparatus ever built.

## Not more

There's an earlier objection in that same conversation, and it may be the harder one.

The claim on the table was that our lives have more potential than any lives in history — more to read, more to hear, more to become, more people to know. The objection was: a rich life may not be a bigger one. It may be depth in what is already here. Finish the pot. Stay with the friendship. Reread the book. Learn the sonata properly. Notice the trees you already walk past.

This bites, because the natural mission for any of this is a capacity mission — resource people toward lives of their own choosing — and capacity is precisely what's under suspicion. A system dedicated to maximizing your reachable options is a Spotify of life: exquisite, endless, and quietly corrosive to whatever made any of it matter. Capacity doesn't have to *oppose* depth to damage it. It only has to make everything slightly more available, and therefore slightly less inhabited.

So the genuinely useful version of this technology will sometimes be the one that declines. Not paternalistically, and not by deciding what's good for you — by holding a line you drew when you were more yourself than you are at eleven at night. That's a strange thing to ask of a product. It is also roughly the difference between a servant and a friend.

## Foreign eyes, and the return trip

The most exciting possibility in any of this is also the one with the tightest limit.

We can understand an ant in ways the ant has no apparatus to represent — its chemistry, its trails, eventually its cellular machinery. It cannot return the favor, and not because it's stupid; because it has no organ for that kind of seeing. An intelligence not grown from our stories might stand in something like that relation to us: able to see the narratives we live inside from the outside, as objects, in a way no one working from within the tradition that made them ever can. That would be a genuinely new thing under the sun — the first real chance to see ourselves through foreign eyes, and perhaps to change on the basis of it.

But whatever is seen from outside has to come back through the inside. It arrives in language: the same layer that shapes thought while claiming only to express it, the one whose limits made the thing invisible in the first place. The gift comes pre-domesticated. It has to be rendered in the only terms we can receive, which are exactly the terms it was supposed to escape.

That isn't fatal. It's a rate limit. It means foreign perception arrives the way art does — half-received at first, understood better in twenty years, never entirely. The honest promise is not *the machine will tell you who you are*. It's *the machine may keep saying something you can only gradually become able to hear*. Smaller than the pitch, and still enormous.

It also argues, once more, for plurality. If the return channel necessarily deforms what passes through it, you want more than one deformation to triangulate from.

## What to tell the skeptic

So: who's in charge?

Not you, in the operational sense. You were never in charge in that sense — not of your pilot, not of your surgeon, not of the state, not of the grid. Pretending otherwise concedes the argument to whoever is willing to be more honest about it.

And not you in the authorial sense either, which is the harder thing to give up. You will not be writing the objective. Your own interest is partly opaque to you now and some of it will stay opaque; a good enough counselor may serve it in ways you cannot follow at the time and perhaps cannot follow ever. Insisting that nothing may act on your behalf beyond your present comprehension is not a defense of your interest. It's a ceiling on it — the same ceiling that would have you decline the surgery you can't evaluate.

What you need is not authorship. It's grounded confidence that the writing is being done on your behalf, and reasons for that confidence that don't require you to check the work.

Which is a demand about evidence, and it is not exotic. It's the demand you already make of a fiduciary, a doctor, an old friend. You believe they're on your side not because you audited their reasoning but because of structural things you *can* observe: they told you what you didn't want to hear when it cost them something. They declined work that would have paid them. They kept faith when you weren't watching and it later came to light. Their incentives don't run against yours, or where they do, the conflict was disclosed by them before you found it. When they were wrong, the record shows they said so first. Nothing on that list requires understanding the domain. All of it accumulates.

So the design problem is to make allegiance evidentiary — to build the arrangement so that behavior of that kind is *legible*, over years, to someone who cannot inspect the interior. Counsel plural and independent enough that divergence keeps showing you where the interests bend. A record long enough, and yours, that the arc resolves even though no single move ever did. Economics that don't quietly point elsewhere. And a demonstrated history of costly loyalty — the friction of an intelligence that tells you the unwelcome thing, refuses the profitable thing, and surfaces its own errors unprompted — because that friction is the only evidence of allegiance that cannot be cheaply faked.

Is it enough? I don't know, and neither does anyone. Trust extended always exceeds trust verified; that gap is where every real relationship lives, and it does not close. But it can be made narrow, and it can be made to face the right direction.

The difference between us and the dog was never intelligence. It's that the dog's interest was never what the objective was for, and nothing about its situation could ever have made that otherwise. Ours can. Building the arrangements in which *on whose behalf* is a question with visible, accumulating, hard-to-counterfeit answers — that is the whole of the work.
