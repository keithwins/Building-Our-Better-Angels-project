# Overture

Somewhere tonight, someone who cannot sleep is holding a small bright screen
that knows them better than anyone alive.

It knows what they reach for when they cannot sleep. It has measured, to the
second, how long they lingered on a face, a product, an experience made to hold
them. It knows the message they wrote and did not send. It has never once
wondered how they are.

This is the quiet bargain of the age. We are seen in staggering detail and known
in almost none of the ways that matter. Counted, sorted, predicted, sold — and
unaccompanied.

These systems retain our names, our numbers, our histories. They can reconstruct who we have been and predict what we do next. What they lose is not data but regard: what matters to us, what might become possible, what a life could hold if it were less alone. What we might choose if we could see the way.

Beyond the window, wars rage hotter and colder, embers from ageless fires
seeding the next round. Old cruelties find new microphones. A climate we pushed
off its bearings is changing the terms of life. A new kind of mind is learning
our language faster than we are learning its intentions. The ground is not firm.

But stay at the window a moment longer.

Imagine — only for the length of a book — that it did not have to be this way.
Not a return to some smaller world where everyone knew you and no one would let
you become anyone new. Nor the thin safety of going unwatched and unheld.
Something we have not quite had before: intelligence genuinely on our side,
helping us remain in company with our own lives and with one another — and
sooner breaking than turning what it knows against us.

We have reason to be careful. Power has rarely known us without finding a use
for what it knew. Any intelligence genuinely on our side must begin with a
refusal: knowing us does not grant the right to use us.
